Report card: how accurate each pollster was on Oct 4

Each pollster's last poll before the vote, compared with the real result. Too low on Flávio always means too high on Lula by the same amount.

Why "share of the two"? On Oct 4 there were 12 candidates. In the runoff there are only two. To compare the old polls with the runoff fairly, we look only at the votes for Flávio and Lula and ask how they split. In reality Flávio got 47.03% and Lula 45.16%. Of those 92.19 points, Flávio had 51.0% and Lula 49.0%. Datafolha said Flávio 42 and Lula 45, which is 48.3% vs 51.7% of the two, so it had Flávio 2.7 points too low and Lula 2.7 too high. That gap is what the adjustment uses.

Vote math from the real Oct 4 count

This part doesn't use runoff polls as its base. It starts from the real Oct 4 votes, adds where the eliminated candidates' voters say they're going, then applies how turnout has changed between rounds in past elections. It assumes each finalist keeps their own Oct 4 voters.

Step 1: the eliminated candidates' voters

Where their voters say they're going, according to

Endorsements don't carry voters with them. Cury endorsed Flávio, yet AtlasIntel finds more of his voters going to Lula. Endorsements: Caiado and Zema backed Flávio, Cury backed him with conditions, Renan Santos backed nobody.

Step 2: turnout

On Oct 4, 33.4 million of 158.7 million registered voters stayed home (21.08%, the most since 1998). In five of the last six runoffs, even more people stayed home in the second round.

Why it usually rises: fewer races on the ballot. This year 21 of 27 states already chose their governor (a record), including Lula's strongest states like Bahia and Piauí and big Flávio states like São Paulo and Rio, so voters there have one less reason to go. 2022 was the exception, when both campaigns pushed turnout hard and some cities ran free buses.

Step 3: region by region

National polls also report results by region. This takes Datafolha's regional numbers (Oct 13–15) and applies them to how many people really voted in each region on Oct 4, so a region counts as much as its real number of voters. It follows the adjustment slider in the runoff polls section, and the vote totals follow the typical-turnout case above.

How to read it: compare "Oct 4 share" with "Datafolha now". That shows who gained in each region since the first round. "Projected votes" go up for both candidates everywhere because they now include the 9.3 million votes from eliminated candidates and the change in turnout, so look at the shares, not the vote totals. Datafolha reports Norte and Centro-Oeste together, so both use the same number. National polls don't publish state results; state-level numbers would need separate state polls (São Paulo, Minas Gerais, Rio and Bahia hold about 45% of voters).

States and governors

On Oct 4, 21 of the 27 states already elected their governor, a record. Voters there have only the presidential race left, which usually means more people stay home.

Does it help one side? Less than it seems. The 21 decided states include Lula's strongest (Bahia, Piauí, Ceará, Pernambuco, Maranhão) and Flávio's biggest (São Paulo, Minas Gerais, Rio, Paraná, Rio Grande do Sul, Santa Catarina, Goiás), so where people stay home roughly cancels out: on its own it's worth only a few hundredths of a point. What matters more is who stays home. Poorer, less engaged voters skip runoffs most, and they lean Lula. Our "more of Lula's voters stay home" scenario in the vote math puts that at about 0.3 points for Flávio. Voting is compulsory in Brazil, which keeps the gap between richer and poorer turnout smaller than in countries where it's optional.

      How the numbers work

      • Our projection: the average of three estimates (adjusted polls at 50%, the vote math with typical turnout, and the sum of regions), plus half of this week's poll trend carried forward to Sunday. Chance of winning assumes the final result can land about 2 points either side of the projection (one standard error), which is roughly how far final polls missed in recent Brazilian elections. It uses the default settings, so moving the sliders doesn't change it.
      • Average of the polls: the latest runoff poll from each pollster, all counted equally, with nothing changed.
      • Adjusted polls: for each pollster, we take how far off it was on Flávio's share of the two on Oct 4 and add back the percentage you pick. Lula gets whatever is left out of 100.
      • Most accurate pollsters: those within 1 point of Flávio's real share of the two on Oct 4, with nothing changed.
      • Turnout: the median change in abstention between rounds across the six runoffs from 2002 to 2022 (+2.0 points), applied to the 158.7 million registered voters. When more people vote than on Oct 4, the extra voters split the way people who skipped Oct 4 say they'll vote.
      • Regions: Oct 4 votes for Flávio and Lula in each region, scaled to the typical-turnout total, times Datafolha's regional share of the two (adjusted by Datafolha's own Oct 4 mistake when the slider is above 0%).
      • Betting odds: Polymarket snapshots reported by the Rio Times; the market's own history can differ slightly.
      • Debate comments: 12,995 top-level comments on 11 clips, fetched Oct 20 at 03:55 UTC through YouTube's official API. Each comment was read and tagged by Claude. Bot flags: same text from 3+ accounts, a short slogan repeated by 5+ accounts, an account posting 5+ times, links or self-promotion, or coordination spotted while reading.

      Limits: part of the Oct 4 polling miss came from Caiado and Renan Santos voters switching to Flávio at the last minute, which can't happen the same way in a two-person race. BTG/Nexus's last poll before Oct 4 was from Sep 28, so its error includes a real late swing. Ipespe, Data Ranking, Alfa Inteligência, DataTrends and Indexa have no Oct 4 poll here and get the average mistake. Veritá's runoff polls (Oct 17, and Oct 21 with Flávio at 50.7%) are left out because their full reports couldn't be checked. Quaest's Oct 17–20 transfer numbers don't cover Zema's voters, so that row keeps Quaest's Oct 10–13 figures. The voter-transfer percentages also come from polls.
